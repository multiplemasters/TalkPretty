Leadership · Conflict · DBT

DEARMAN

A structured way to make a request, set a boundary, disagree, or advocate for yourself while staying clear, calm, and collaborative.

D — DescribeState observable facts.
E — ExpressShare your feelings or concern.
A — AssertClearly ask for what you need.
R — ReinforceExplain the benefit.
M — MindfulStay on the main point.
A — Appear confidentUse steady, direct delivery.
N — NegotiateLook for workable alternatives.

How to use it

Start with what happened rather than your interpretation of someone's motives. Explain why it matters, make one clear request, and then resist getting pulled into side arguments. If the other person cannot meet the exact request, negotiate the underlying need.

Design leadership example“The scope has changed three times since we started the sprint. I’m concerned that we’re trading away craft and creating rework. I’d like us to agree on the top priority before adding new requirements. That will help the team move faster and produce a stronger outcome.”
Watch out: DEARMAN is not a script for winning. Avoid turning “Describe” into blame or “Assert” into an ultimatum.

Best for

Boundaries, stakeholder conflict, negotiation, difficult 1:1s, saying no, and advocating for your team.

Persuasion · UX Writing · Conversion

CTA — Call To Action

A CTA is the explicit next step you want the audience to take. It turns communication from information into movement.

How to use it

Make the action specific, easy to understand, and appropriately timed. A strong CTA answers: What should happen next? In product design, the CTA can be a button; in leadership, it can be a decision or commitment.

Weak: “Let’s keep the conversation going.”
Stronger: “Approve the research plan by Friday so we can recruit Monday.”

Best for

Presentations, emails, product flows, executive asks, campaign messaging, and meeting close-outs.

Feedback · Coaching · Leadership

SBI — Situation, Behavior, Impact

A feedback model that keeps the conversation anchored in a specific event, observable behavior, and resulting impact rather than personality or intent.

SituationWhen and where it happened.
BehaviorWhat you could actually observe.
ImpactWhat happened as a result.

How to use it

Describe the moment as if a camera recorded it. Avoid labels such as “careless,” “negative,” or “not strategic.” Then explain the effect on the work, team, customer, or relationship. SBI can be positive or corrective.

Coaching example“In yesterday’s design critique (Situation), you interrupted two teammates while they were explaining their rationale (Behavior). It made it harder for the group to explore the ideas fully (Impact).”
Leadership tip: After SBI, add a forward-looking question: “What could you do differently next time?” This turns feedback into coaching.

Best for

Performance conversations, praise, coaching, design critique, collaboration issues, and behavior change.

Storytelling · Interviews

CAR — Challenge, Action, Result

A compact story structure that makes an accomplishment easy to understand without overloading the listener with background.

ChallengeWhat problem or constraint existed?
ActionWhat did you personally do?
ResultWhat changed?

How to use it

Keep the Challenge brief, spend the most time on your decisions and actions, then land on measurable results. CAR is particularly useful when you have limited time and want the listener to remember your contribution.

Example“Referral completion had plateaued (Challenge). I led a cross-functional redesign that simplified the flow and clarified the value proposition (Action). The new experience increased referral conversion and created a stronger foundation for global expansion (Result).”

Best for

Portfolio stories, interviews, executive updates, case studies, and self-promotion.

Interviews · Storytelling

STAR — Situation, Task, Action, Result

A behavioral interview framework that gives a complete narrative while making your individual contribution explicit.

SituationSet the context.
TaskClarify your responsibility or goal.
ActionExplain what you did.
ResultShow the outcome and learning.

How to use it

Don't spend 80% of your answer on context. The interviewer is usually evaluating your judgment, behavior, and impact, so emphasize Action and Result. Use “I” when describing your contribution even when the work was collaborative.

Interview prompt: “Tell me about a time you influenced without authority.” Structure the answer around the situation, what you were responsible for, the specific influence tactics you used, and the measurable or observable result.

Best for

Behavioral interviews, promotion cases, leadership stories, and portfolio presentations.

Persuasion · Marketing · Storytelling

AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

A persuasion sequence that moves an audience from noticing something to caring about it, wanting the outcome, and taking a next step.

AttentionGive them a reason to notice.
InterestExplain why it matters.
DesireMake the benefit compelling.
ActionMake the next step clear.

How to use it

Lead with the problem or insight that earns attention. Build relevance with evidence or a story. Translate features into outcomes people want. Finish with a specific action.

Product pitch“Referral growth has stalled. Research shows customers struggle to understand the value before they share. We can surface that value earlier and reduce friction in the flow. Let’s approve a 10% experiment next sprint.”

Best for

Presentations, product pitches, landing pages, campaigns, UX copy, and persuasive storytelling.

Writing · Essays · Reports

TEEL — Topic, Explanation, Evidence, Link

A paragraph-level writing structure that makes an argument easy to follow and supports claims with evidence.

TopicState the main point.
ExplanationClarify what you mean.
EvidenceSupport it with proof.
LinkConnect back to the argument.

How to use it

Think of each paragraph as doing one job. Start with the point, explain its significance, provide evidence, and then connect it to the broader argument or next point.

Design strategy example“Customers need clearer referral value. Interviews show uncertainty about the reward is a major hesitation. In five interviews, participants asked about the reward before sharing. This suggests value clarity should move earlier in the journey.”

Best for

Strategy documents, research readouts, essays, recommendations, and persuasive written arguments.

Sales · Discovery · Consulting

SPIN — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-Payoff

A consultative questioning framework designed to uncover the customer's real problem and connect it to the value of a solution.

SituationUnderstand the current state.
ProblemIdentify pain or friction.
ImplicationExplore why it matters.
Need-PayoffSurface the value of change.

How to use it

Ask rather than tell. Move from facts to consequences, then let the other person articulate what improvement would be worth. In design, this can be adapted for stakeholder discovery.

Stakeholder discovery“How are teams handling this today? Where does the process break down? What happens when that occurs? If we removed that friction, what would it enable?”

Best for

Discovery, stakeholder interviews, consulting, sales conversations, and problem framing.

Conflict · Assertiveness · Boundaries

DESC — Describe, Express, Specify, Consequences

An assertive communication structure for addressing conflict, stating a need, and making the consequences or benefits explicit.

DescribeState the facts.
ExpressState how you feel or why it matters.
SpecifySay what you want to change.
ConsequencesExplain likely outcomes.

How to use it

DESC is especially useful when the behavior needs to change. It overlaps with DEARMAN but is more compact and often used as a direct conflict script.

Example“When project decisions are changed after design sign-off, it creates rework. I’m concerned about quality and team capacity. I’d like us to document changes and revisit them at the next planning checkpoint. That will make trade-offs visible and protect delivery.”

Best for

Conflict resolution, boundaries, performance issues, and recurring collaboration problems.

Executive Communication · Opinions

PREP — Point, Reason, Example, Point

A concise structure for answering a question or making a recommendation without wandering.

PointLead with your answer.
ReasonExplain why.
ExampleMake it concrete.
PointLand the takeaway.

How to use it

Put the conclusion first. This is particularly powerful with senior leaders who want the answer before the background. Use one strong example rather than a collection of weak ones.

Question: “Should we invest in this?” “Yes, but only as a focused experiment (Point). The evidence suggests the opportunity is real, but uncertainty remains (Reason). Our last test increased engagement while exposing a retention issue (Example). So I recommend a limited pilot with a clear success threshold (Point).”

Best for

Executive communication, meetings, interviews, recommendations, and Q&A.

Strategy · Planning

SWOT — Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats

A simple strategic lens for separating internal capabilities from external conditions.

StrengthsInternal advantages.
WeaknessesInternal limitations.
OpportunitiesExternal openings.
ThreatsExternal risks.

How to use it

Don't treat SWOT as the strategy itself. Use it to surface assumptions, then turn the findings into choices: what to exploit, improve, pursue, or protect against.

Design team example Strength: deep customer knowledge. Weakness: fragmented research. Opportunity: a shared insight repository. Threat: duplicated agency work and competing priorities.

Best for

Team planning, product strategy, career planning, competitive analysis, and organizational discussions.

Handoffs · Executive Updates · Clarity

SBAR — Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation

A concise structure for communicating a current situation, the relevant context, your assessment, and the action you recommend.

SituationWhat is happening now?
BackgroundWhat context matters?
AssessmentWhat do you believe is happening?
RecommendationWhat should happen next?

How to use it

SBAR originated in healthcare handoffs but is useful anywhere decisions need to happen quickly. Keep Background selective: only include context that changes the decision.

Executive update “The launch is at risk (Situation). Engineering lost two days to a dependency and research found a major usability issue (Background). I believe the current date creates unacceptable quality risk (Assessment). I recommend moving the launch one week and using the time for a targeted fix (Recommendation).”

Best for

Status updates, escalation, incident communication, handoffs, and decision-making.

Reflection · Retrospectives · Learning

3-2-1 Reflection

A lightweight reflection format commonly adapted as three takeaways, two interesting observations, and one question or next action.

3Key things learned.
2Interesting observations.
1Question, insight, or action.

How to use it

The exact meaning can vary by context, so define the three numbers before using it with a team. Its value is forcing synthesis rather than collecting a long list of notes.

Project retro “3 things we learned: users need clearer value, stakeholders need earlier visibility, and our prototype testing was too late. 2 observations: the strongest concept was also the simplest; agency collaboration accelerated exploration. 1 action: establish an earlier prototype checkpoint.”

Best for

Retrospectives, workshops, learning sessions, research debriefs, and personal reflection.

Feedback · Coaching

COIN — Context, Observation, Impact, Next Steps

A coaching-oriented feedback model that adds a future-facing next step to the classic observation-and-impact pattern.

ContextSet the situation.
ObservationDescribe what happened.
ImpactExplain the effect.
Next StepsAgree on what to do differently.

How to use it

COIN is useful when you want the conversation to end with a behavioral commitment. It works particularly well in recurring coaching because the “Next Steps” can be revisited later.

Manager example “In the last two project reviews (Context), I noticed you presented the solution before showing the research evidence (Observation). Senior partners challenged the recommendation before understanding the customer problem (Impact). Next time, lead with the evidence and invite questions before presenting the solution (Next Steps).”

Best for

Manager coaching, development plans, performance conversations, and reinforcing good habits.

Executive Storytelling · Strategy

SCQA — Situation, Complication, Question, Answer

A problem-framing structure often associated with consulting communication. It creates a narrative tension before delivering the recommendation.

SituationEstablish the known context.
ComplicationIntroduce what changed or went wrong.
QuestionSurface the decision that follows.
AnswerDeliver the recommendation.

How to use it

Keep the Situation familiar and concise. The Complication should create a genuine need for a decision. The Question can be explicit or implicit, but the Answer should be crisp and directly address it.

Strategy example “Referral traffic continues to grow (Situation), but completion has plateaued as the journey becomes more complex across markets (Complication). How do we unlock the next phase of growth without adding operational complexity (Question)? Simplify the core journey first, then scale market-specific enhancements (Answer).”

Best for

Executive decks, strategy proposals, case studies, board-level communication, and problem statements.

Persuasion · Product · Sales

FAB — Features, Advantages, Benefits

A messaging framework that translates what something is into why it is useful and, ultimately, why the audience should care.

FeatureWhat it has or does.
AdvantageWhat the feature enables.
BenefitWhy that matters to the person.

How to use it

Move progressively from product language to human value. “Feature” is about capability; “Advantage” is about utility; “Benefit” connects that utility to an outcome the audience values.

Product example “The dashboard shows referral performance by market (Feature). Leaders can compare performance without manually combining reports (Advantage). They can spot where to invest and act faster (Benefit).”

Best for

Product pitches, marketing, UX messaging, sales enablement, and explaining design decisions.

Copywriting · Persuasion

PASTOR — Problem, Amplify, Story/Solution, Transformation, Offer, Response

A long-form persuasion structure that builds empathy around a problem, presents a solution, shows the desired transformation, and asks for a response.

ProblemName the pain.
AmplifyShow why it matters.
Story / SolutionIntroduce the answer.
TransformationPaint the better future.
OfferMake the value concrete.
ResponseAsk for action.

How to use it

PASTOR is most useful when the audience needs emotional context before they are ready to act. The “Amplify” step should create relevance, not fear-mongering. The “Transformation” step should make the outcome tangible.

Example Start with the customer problem, demonstrate the cost of leaving it unresolved, introduce the solution through a credible story, show what improves, make the offer specific, and end with one clear response.

Best for

Long-form copy, campaigns, sales pages, fundraising, product narratives, and persuasive storytelling.
